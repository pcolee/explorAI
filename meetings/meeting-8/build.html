Step 1: give it context first

The AI only knows your class if you tell it. Grab one or two of these before you paste a prompt:

  • Syllabus: the grading breakdown (what percent homework, quizzes, exams are worth).
  • Your scores so far, from Canvas.
  • Notes or a study guide for the class you’re most worried about.
  • Your finals schedule: each class, day, and time.

No materials with you? Use the sample syllabus and notes at the bottom.

Publishing makes it public. If you plan to publish, build the app so you type your scores or notes into the app, not into the prompt. Then the link is safe to share and works for any class.

Step 2: pick a prompt

Every class

“What do I need on the final?” calculator

Shows the score you need on the final for each letter grade.

Here is my class's grading breakdown from the syllabus: [paste]. My current scores are: [paste]. Build me an interactive calculator that shows what I need on the final to get an A, B, or C. Let me edit any score and update the results live. Keep the design clean and simple.
Review

Study game generator

Turns your notes into a game for solo or study-group review.

Here are my notes for [class]: [paste]. Turn them into a playable [Jeopardy board / timed quiz / flashcard boss battle] with at least 15 questions. Track my score and show me which topics I missed at the end.
Practice

Mock exam simulator

A timed practice exam in your professor’s style that grades your answers.

Act as my [class] professor. Using this study guide and these past exam questions: [paste], build a timed 20-minute practice exam with a mix of question types. When I submit, grade it, explain each wrong answer, and list the topics I should review.
Focus

Focus station

A personal finals-week page for staying on track.

Build me a finals-week focus page with: a Pomodoro timer (25/5, adjustable), a countdown to each of my finals [list each class, date, and time], and a "currently working on" box. Make it calm and minimal. Optional: add a button that opens my focus playlist: [paste a Spotify or YouTube link].
Yours

Your own idea

Anything that makes finals week easier: a meal plan, a sleep schedule, a group study planner…

✎ Editable: type right in the box. It stays here if you close the page.

Step 3: fix it by saying what’s wrong

Your first version will be rough. That’s normal: round two is where it gets good. Describe bugs like you’d tell a tutor: what you did, what you expected, what happened instead.

Bug report

When something’s broken

When I [what I did], I expected [what should happen], but [what happened instead]. Please fix only that.
Save my progress so it's still there when I close the page and come back.
Make it work well on my phone screen.
Make the questions harder, and explain each answer after I pick one.
Add a dark mode toggle.
Add a reset button that clears everything.
Let me type my own scores or notes into the app instead of having them built in.
  1. One change per message. If five changes break it, you won’t know which one did.
  2. Click every button. The AI can say a feature works when it doesn’t. You’re the tester.
  3. A change broke it? Restore the previous version, then ask for one smaller change.

Step 4: keep it

Don’t leave it stuck in one tab. Do at least one of these before you go:

  1. Find it later. Every app you build is listed at aistudio.google.com/apps. Bookmark it.
  2. Share it. Your app is private until you share it. People you share with can use it and see its code, and what they use counts toward your limits.
  3. Publish it. Click Publish (top right). Then Get started if you see it, Continue, Continue with free URL, and Publish your app. You get a link ending in .ai.studio that anyone can open.
  4. Put it on your phone. Open your link on your phone. iPhone (Safari): tap Share, then Add to Home Screen. Android (Chrome): tap the ⋮ menu, then Add to Home screen.
Free accounts can publish up to 2 apps, so publish your best one. If Publish asks for a billing account, your account isn’t eligible for the free tier (school accounts aren’t): share it instead, or bookmark your apps page. Published means public: no passwords, private info, or anyone else’s info in it.

Intro to Psychology · Memory unit

Memory has three stages: encoding (getting information in), storage (keeping it), and retrieval (getting it back out).
Sensory memory holds what we see and hear for a second or two.
Short-term memory holds about 7 items, plus or minus 2 (George Miller, 1956), for around 20 seconds unless we rehearse them.
Chunking groups items into bigger units, like reading a phone number as 3 chunks, so more fits in short-term memory.
Working memory (Baddeley and Hitch) is short-term memory as an active workspace: it holds and works with information at the same time.
Long-term memory is explicit (facts and events you can say out loud) or implicit (skills and habits, like riding a bike).
Explicit memory includes episodic memory (personal events) and semantic memory (general facts).
The hippocampus helps form new explicit memories.
Serial position effect: we remember the first items in a list (primacy) and the last items (recency) best.
Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve: we forget most new information quickly, then forgetting slows down.
Spacing effect: studying in several short sessions beats one long cram session.
Testing effect: quizzing yourself helps you remember more than rereading.
Proactive interference: old information gets in the way of learning new information.
Retroactive interference: new information gets in the way of remembering old information.
